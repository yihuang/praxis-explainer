Lock a secret
inside a problem

If encryption needed no key, but was instead made to a "condition" — whoever can make that condition hold can read it — what would the world look like? For thirteen years, the idea has been "sound in theory, absurd in engineering." Praxis moved it from "physically impossible" to "a few seconds on a laptop."

Act I

Encrypt to a problem, not to a person

Every encryption in use today has a key behind it. Either you hand the key to the other party, or you keep it yourself. Either way, something has to be transmitted, stored, and trusted.

In 2013, Garg, Gentry, Sahai, and Waters proposed a strange kind of encryption: no key. Lock the message inside a statement, and the statement looks like this—

“there exists a w such that program P outputs 1 on input w”

Such a w is called a witness in theoretical computer science. The rule is simple to the point of brutality:

  • whoever holds this w can open it;
  • whoever does not — even with the ciphertext, all the public parameters, and all the computing power in the world — cannot read a single bit.

It is called witness encryption. The ciphertext carries its own "release condition"; there is no key distribution, and no party is trusted to release the message.

Imagine a letter addressed to the future: once it is sent, the sender dies, the server shuts down, the company goes bankrupt — as long as the letter's condition can still be satisfied, it sits there, waiting for whoever can produce the answer. This is not a metaphor; it is the literal function of the technique.

Act II

A triangle stuck for thirteen years

For a cryptographic scheme to be really used, three wishes have to come true at once.

  • General: it can encrypt to an arbitrary NP statement — a Turing machine, a smart contract, a piece of Rust code — not just one particular format.
  • Provable: security rests on some widely accepted model and assumption, not on "nobody has broken it yet."
  • Practical: ciphertext size, encryption time, and decryption time are numbers you can discuss.

For thirteen years, every known scheme has held at most two of those edges:

General + provable — from indistinguishability obfuscation (iO), from some assumption about linear codes, from Hair and Sahai's 2026 "rank gap" theorem. These are all feasibility results: they exist mathematically, and are astronomical in engineering.

Provable + practical — they cover only very narrow relations: witness encryption for signatures, witness encryption for openings of KZG commitments. Secure, but not general enough.

General + practical — resting on heuristic arguments or assumptions made to measure. A good part of these have already been broken, or shown to allow decryption that bypasses the witness outright.

Praxis's ambition is to stand in the middle of the triangle and, for the first time, have all three be true at once.

Act III

The core mechanism: turn "solving equations" into "the rank of a matrix"

This is the heart of the whole paper. Go slowly.

Any NP statement can be written as a pile of quadratic equations over Boolean unknowns. Take N unknowns that are 0 or 1, w₁…w_N, then fix w₀ = 1, and every constraint becomes "two linear forms multiplied together equal a third linear form" — this is called R1CS, the common language of the zero-knowledge world.

Now make one move: turn the vector w into a matrix.

W = w · wᵀ　　(the (i, j) entry is just w_i w_j)

This is called the moment matrix. It looks like nothing but products piled up, yet it does something remarkable: any quadratic form a(w)·b(w) can be written as ⟨C, W⟩, a "linear combination of W", where C is a public matrix.

So the whole statement is translated into one sentence:

"w satisfies all constraints"　⟺　"W passes all linear tests"

And the W that a genuine witness gives, W = wwᵀ, has rank exactly 1: every row is a multiple of w, or else plain zero.

The trouble is exactly here. The tests are linear, so "the matrices that pass" form a linear space — and things that are not witnesses get mixed into it. The paper calls them cheat. The mildest example: two assignments u and u′ each fall a little short, but their errors on each constraint have opposite signs; average them, (uuᵀ + u′u′ᵀ)/2, the errors cancel, every test passes — and its rank is 2.

The whole paper is, in essence, about managing this rank. In the workbench below, all four of these steps come with concrete numbers.

Act IV

Hide it in the exponent

Once rank gives you a handle, the next step is to turn it into encryption. The trick is the oldest one in cryptography: put the value into the exponent of a group element.

Take a group 𝔾 of prime order q and write v·g as [v]. You are handed a pile of [T_ij], and you can add them up with any coefficients you like — group operations are yours to perform — yet you cannot see what values are inside. You can combine; you cannot peek.

The encryptor picks a hidden vector y, a hidden vector r of small integers, and a test mask M, then stuffs the key κ into the corner of the table and assembles one:

T = ½(y rᵀ + r yᵀ) + M + κ·e₀e₀ᵀ

The ciphertext publishes every [T_ij]. Anyone — including someone with no idea what the witness is — can weight-combine those entries along an arbitrary matrix W and obtain a group element:

⟨T, W⟩ = yᵀWr + ⟨M, W⟩ + κ·W₀₀

Each of the three terms has a job:

  • The third term is the key. At the witness's matrix corner W₀₀ = w₀² = 1, so this term is exactly κ.
  • The second term is the guard. If W passes every test, the whole term is 0. If not, it is a uniformly random scalar — and the entire combination collapses into noise on the spot.
  • The first term is the lock. It stands in front of the key, and it is the one thing that nothing but guessing can take apart.

Note the elegance of the trick: nobody needs to know the witness in order to encrypt. The encryptor need not even know whether the problem has a solution. He merely follows the public tests and builds a table that no one can read, but that the right person can combine correctly.

Concretely? In the workbench, the corner of this table is T₀₀ = 75 + 100 + 13 = 188 ≡ 87 (mod 101) — three pieces added together, and one cell is done.

Act V

That lock is really just one integer

In the witness's hands, the picture collapses, almost miraculously.

Substitute W = wwᵀ and the first term becomes yᵀ(wwᵀ)r = (y·w)(r·w) = ψ · r(w). Here ψ = y(w) is something the witness can compute for itself — it knows w, and the ciphertext holds every [y_i]. But r(w) = Σ w_i r_i is an integer: it sits somewhere inside a known interval, and the witness does not know where.

So decryption reduces to one task: find an integer.

How wide is the interval? Each r_i takes its value in [0, g) independently, and r(w) is the sum of those r_i whose index i is a nonzero position of w — worst case the interval is N·g wide. Try it one value at a time and you are in for a nightmare.

But you can search in two directions at once. Starting at the center c_w, run a baby-step giant-step search outward: first lay down a table of √ baby steps, then leap across in giant steps. The cost of finding it is roughly

O( √|r(w) − c_w| )　≈　O( 2^(λ/4) · N^(1/4) )

This is the search the paper calls exponential but concretely short. At λ = 100: the range is g ≈ 250.6, yet the search needs only about 229.4 steps — a few hundred million, at 6 field multiplications each. One laptop, a few seconds.

The whole pipeline — table, combination, recovery, search — is walked through from scratch in the workbench with concrete 𝔽₁₀₁ numbers, and every step can be checked by hand.

And the interval is that narrow only because the unknowns are restricted to Boolean values. The constraint that every number must be 0 or 1 is the technical precondition that makes the search short — which is why the whole scheme insists on writing the relation as a Boolean quadratic system first.

Workbench

Twelve steps, building the encryption from scratch

The previous acts described the mechanism. This section is the ledger: one toy instance, computed all the way from a line of code to recovery.

First, where this ledger comes from. The mask plus a search term plus a bounded search is the rank template from the 2026 Hair–Sahai feasibility result; Praxis merely rewrites it more tidily. Putting the key in the corner, and the α copies, are Praxis's additions. And Praxis's real main course — factoring the table with a QAP, binding the copies, sharing factors — is the four steps of Act VII, the ones that squeeze 10⁴³ GiB down to 7.88 GiB. This section builds up to the template and stops there, because past that point you need polynomials and pairings.

Swap the field from q ≈ 2²⁵³·⁶ to 𝔽₁₀₁, swap 390,000 unknowns for 4 — and not one character of the formulas changes. The only reason to choose 101 is to keep every intermediate result small enough to write on paper.

Anyone who writes code knows the feeling: if you cannot implement a thing from scratch, you do not really understand it. So nothing here is quoted as a conclusion. Every step is derived from the line above it, and every number can be checked against its definition.

Act VI

Rank decides how many numbers you must guess at once

Now consider the adversary. It will not politely combine along wwᵀ; it can combine along any matrix W that passes the tests.

If W has rank d, then W = Σ u_ℓ v_ℓᵀ, so

yᵀWr = Σ (yᵀu_ℓ)(v_ℓᵀr)

It splits into a sum of d products, every factor independent and random, and they do not cancel one another. The only way to break the lock is to guess d integers correctly all at once. The chance of that is at most g−d.

And there is the lever the whole design turns on:

  • We want the easiest-to-guess cheat to have the highest possible rank, say d = 4. Then the range need only be g⁴ ≈ 2^(2λ), that is, g ≈ 2^(λ/2).
  • Honest decryption still guesses only 1 number, at a cost of √g ≈ 2^(λ/4).

The honest and the cheating party each face a different d: one need guess 1 number, the other must guess 4. That is why g can be as large as 2λ/2 — for the honest party it is one √ search, for the cheat it is a one-in-g⁴ stroke of luck.

So how do you lift the minimum rank to 4? Copy the table several times, weight each copy by a polynomial in w, then bind the copies together with hidden linear identities. Repetition without binding is useless: the cheat can plant the same cheating matrix in every copy.

Act VII

Four Surgical Steps: From 10⁴³ to 8 GiB

The Hair–Sahai construction proved that a rank gap is possible. But at its optimum, encrypting a 100-bit key yields a ciphertext of 1.1 × 10¹¹ GiB — not slow, but physically impossible.

Praxis did not reach for "better constants". It made four structural changes. The paper records the cost of each step in a single table:

  • ① Keep only one table. Instead of straining to raise the rank, fix the rank gap at 1 to 2 and enlarge the range g to 2λ. Size and encryption become polynomial; the price is that the search becomes exponential — but an exponential that can be concretely very short. Ciphertext: 4.6 × 10⁴³ GiB.
  • ② Split the table into two factors. The "test mask + key" block can be swapped for a single QAP term A(τ)B(τ)ᵀ of rank 1. With a bilinear pairing, the table becomes two factors of width 2 multiplied together, and the ciphertext collapses from N² elements to 4(N+1). Ciphertext: 0.14 GiB.
  • ③ Bind the copies. To lift the minimum rank from 2 to 4, replicate the table several times and weight the copies with polynomials; the cost is a factor of λ² in size. Ciphertext: 8.1 × 10² GiB.
  • ④ Share factors. These copies differ from one another by a single scalar! Lay them out on a grid, and every row and every column shares one factor. Ciphertext: 7.88 GiB.

The real art is steps three and four: loosening "rank" and "size", which had been welded together, so that each pays its own bill.

Act VIII

The Same Problem, the Same Security Level

Put these into a concrete setting: encrypt to a Groth16 verifier over BN254, at 100-bit security. What is encrypted is a 100-bit key.

A 7.88 GiB ciphertext. Set against the 1.1 × 10¹¹ GiB of the base Hair–Sahai construction, that is a gap of more than ten orders of magnitude.

Encryption takes about 234.2 field multiplications (roughly 2 × 10¹⁰), a few seconds on a laptop. The counterpart figure is 2.6 × 10²⁰.

Decryption (with a proof in hand) takes about 233.0 (roughly 8.6 × 10⁹), more than half of it in the search. The counterpart figure is 1.2 × 10¹⁶ — six orders of magnitude apart.

Memory: a peak of about 12 GB for encryption and about 5 GB for decryption; the ciphertext can be streamed, so it never has to fit in memory whole.

What ships is not something that "can only encrypt 100 bits": decryption recovers a group element, a random oracle derives a key of arbitrary length from it, and the rest is handed to symmetric encryption. Cryptography calls this witness key encapsulation (WKEM).

Don't forget what did not change: 7.88 GiB is still large; encryption still takes seconds; and "seconds" is a victory over "impossible", not over "as fast as HTTPS".
Act IX

Why Believe It

The question a cryptography paper most deserves is: "What does your proof assume?"

Praxis proves extractability in the bilinear generic group model plus the random oracle model, and reduces it to the lattice problem Module-SIS. Both idealised models are standard tools, broadly accepted in pairing cryptography — not "we assume it is secure because nobody has broken it", but "inside this model, no adversary escapes".

The concrete parameters are calibrated against the best known attacks: discrete log in the target group of BN254 sits at about 2¹⁰³, the Module-SIS instance at about 2¹⁰³·⁵. The paper is also candid about the loss in the reduction — for the proof itself to deliver 100 bits, the lattice instance has to be roughly another 4.5 bits harder.

Rarer still: they formalised correctness and extractability in Lean 4, at the real parameters, relying only on three standard axioms of classical logic. Machine-checked, not eyeballed.

Then comes a boundary that has to be stated plainly:

The honest-setup assumption. The scheme encrypts to a Groth16 verifier, and Groth16 needs a setup. Whoever keeps the setup's trapdoor can forge a proof for any public input — and thereby open every ciphertext encrypted under that key. This is not an implementation flaw; it is a structural precondition of constructions of this kind.
Act X

What It Means, and What It Does Not

Generality is not rhetoric. Because the scheme encrypts to the verifier of a "universal argument", one fixed key can serve all relations, at the same cost for every one of them.

You can compile a Rust program into a zero-knowledge virtual machine and fold its execution proof into Groth16. "I know an input that makes this program accept" then becomes a witness. You can write a letter to a future person, on the condition that they can supply an input to some piece of code.

What it is not matters just as much:

  • not a silver bullet for "quantum safety" — pairings over BN254 are not quantum-resistant;
  • not a zero-knowledge proof system — it produces no proofs, it only guarantees that "without a witness you cannot read the plaintext";
  • not "lightweight" — a 7.88 GiB ciphertext is no basis for everyday communication.

Its significance lies elsewhere: for thirteen years, "general + provable + computable" had never been true all at once. The road is no longer a pure existence theorem; it has a concrete shape that can be optimised further — QAPs plus pairings, plus a lattice binding, plus a clever search.

The most captivating moments in mathematics are often the ones where it first tells you "it exists", and then tells you "what it looks like".

Glossary

TermMeaning
witnesswitness. The input that makes the statement true — for example the parameters that make the program output 1.
R1CS / BQSRank-1 constraint system / Boolean quadratic system. Writing an NP relation as a system of equations of the form "two linear forms multiplied give a third", with the unknowns taking 0/1.
moment matrixW = wwᵀ. Entry (i,j) is w_i w_j, so every quadratic form becomes a linear function of W.
cheatcheat. A matrix that passes every linear test yet is produced by no witness. Its rank is at least 2.
rank gapThe witness yields rank 1, a cheat at least rank d. That difference is the measure of security.
QAPQuadratic arithmetic program. Uses polynomials to compress "all constraints hold at once" into a single divisibility check.
pairingThe bilinear map e([a]₁,[b]₂)=[ab]_T. It lets "multiply two factors" be done inside the ciphertext.
Module-SISThe short-vector problem on lattices. It keeps an adversary from prising apart the binding between the multiple tables.
generic group modelAn idealised model in which only group operations are allowed and the representation of elements is hidden. Stronger than the standard model, weaker than "no model at all".
